    ROM needed

    The emulator needs the original ROM: 48.rom (16,384 bytes) for the 48K, 128.rom (32,768 bytes) for the 128K. Drop it here, or press EJECT on the recorder to open it; it is remembered in this browser.

    Contents

    Click a chapter, a coloured tab or the turned-up page corners; the arrow keys turn the pages and F1 closes the manual.

    1

    1. Using the emulator

    This booklet is for anyone who has never used a ZX Spectrum, and for those who used one long ago and have forgotten how. Everything on the desk works: the computer, the television, the recorder and the gadgets around them.

    1. 1The computer. Choose it with the television's channel buttons: 48K, + or 128. Changing channel resets it.
    2. 2A tape. Press STOP/EJ on the recorder and pick a .tap or .tzx tape, a .z80 or .sna snapshot, or a ROM. You can also drop files anywhere on the page, or find a game in the software catalogue (F2, an empty place on the rack, or the empty cassette window), or in your own libraries of games: chapter 4 shows how. Every tape you load goes onto your cassette rack, so you can play it again later, even offline.
    3. 3The recorder. With AUTO LOAD on, the tape starts by itself. Otherwise type the load command and press PLAY.

    Loading a program by hand

    On the 48K and the Spectrum+, type

    LOAD ""
    (J, then SYMBOL SHIFT + P twice, then ENTER)

    and press PLAY. The border flashes while the program loads. On the 128, choose Tape Loader from the menu instead.

    Click the cassette to see what is on the tape; click a line to wind the tape to it.

    If something goes wrong

    Press the reset button on the side of the computer (F8). The Multiface's red button stops a game so that you can save it and come back later. Chapter 4 has more help for a game that will not load.

    4. Finding and loading games

    Games come from three places: the software catalogue, with almost everything ever made for the Spectrum; your own libraries, the folders and zips of games you keep; and your cassette rack, where every tape you load is kept.

    The software catalogue

    1. 1Press F2, or click an empty place on the rack, or the empty cassette window.
    2. 2Type part of a title, say jet set, and press ENTER. ZXDB, the database of Spectrum software, and your libraries are searched together.
    3. 3Move with the arrow keys and press ENTER. When a game has several tapes, choose one; the game downloads and loads by itself.

    In the list, titles in black can be loaded; red ones cannot, because their makers do not allow it or they are newer than the archive. A green star marks a game you have in a library: ENTER loads your file. Titles in green are only in your libraries.

    The line under the title bar says where you are, and the line above the keys says what the keys do there. DELETE goes back one step, BREAK goes back to the first page, and BREAK there returns to the Spectrum. You can also click any line.

    Your own libraries

    1. 1Open the catalogue and press DOWN to reach YOUR LIBRARIES.
    2. 2Press F to add a folder of games (on a PC) or Z to add a zip (on any device), and pick it.
    3. 3Search as usual: your games are found too. Or press ENTER on a library to see all its games, and type to narrow the list.

    Only the list of games is kept in the browser, so a library can be as large as you like; the games stay in your folder or zip and are read when you load one. Add each library once on every device you use.

    The browser forgets your files when you leave, so on your next visit your libraries are listed but not connected. Press R (Reconnect to my library) when you want them: Chrome and Edge on a PC ask for permission; other browsers, phones and tablets ask you to pick the zip or folder again. Loading a game from a library that is not connected offers R, or the archive's copy if there is one.

    Collections such as TOSEC keep many copies of each game. You see one line per game: bad copies, and zips with no tape or snapshot inside, are left out, and so are cracked, trained or hacked versions when a clean one exists. If a game has several good versions, ENTER lists them best first.

    The cassette rack

    Every tape you load goes onto the rack, so you can play it again later, even offline. Click a tape to load it. The rack has four banks of 8 places: the buttons A to D under it show the others. MY TAPES lists the bank on show: ENTER loads, DELETE twice removes a tape.

    How fast it loads

    The SPEED switch on the recorder (F9): NORMAL is real tape speed, with the stripes and the screech; FAST runs the computer flat out while the tape plays; INSTANT puts programs straight into memory, and runs games with their own loaders flat out with the picture still, so even those take only seconds.

    Tapes in parts, and multi-load games

    Some games come in parts: two sides, a tape of levels, or a 48K and a 128K version. They go into the recorder as one tape. Click the cassette to see its parts, and click one to wind the tape there; AUTO LOAD starts at the right version for your computer.

    The tape starts by itself when a game listens for it, and stops when the game has what it wants. A game that loads its levels one at a time therefore finds the next one waiting. If the tape runs out while a game still waits, the screen offers to rewind it, wind it to a part, or put in another tape.

    If a game will not load

    • Try another of its tapes in the catalogue; .tzx tapes are the most faithful copies.
    • Some games work only on the 48K: change to channel 1 and load it again.
    • Press F8 to reset, then load the tape again from the rack.
    • A game made for the 128 will not run on the 48K: change to channel 3.
    5

    5. Saving

    Saving onto tape, as in 1982

    1. 1Press REC on the recorder (F5 on a PC). REC and PLAY go down together.
    2. 2Let the game save, or type SAVE in BASIC (chapter 6).
    3. 3Press STOP when it has finished.

    It records onto a tape of your own on the rack: <game> saves while you play a game, or My programs after a reset, for what you write yourself. If you forget REC, the screen asks.

    To load it back, type LOAD "" or choose the game's load option. When the recorder has nothing to load, the screen lets you pick a tape from the rack, or rewind this one.

    Snapshots with the Multiface

    The red button on the Multiface One (F4) freezes the computer at any moment and shows its menu: SAVE downloads a snapshot, a .sna file of everything in the computer's memory; LOAD opens one, and the game carries on exactly where it was. TURBO runs as fast as possible, RESET restarts, and RETURN (or BREAK) goes back to the game.

    Keeping your tapes safe

    The rack lives in this browser on this device. To keep a copy, or to move it to another device, open MY TAPES and press S: a backup file of the whole rack is saved. On the other device, MY TAPES and L load it. Your libraries are not in the backup: they are your own files, so add them on each device.

    6. Programming in BASIC

    Switch on, and the Spectrum is ready for you to write programs in BASIC. This chapter is a first taste; the Spectrum's own manuals go much further.

    Keywords on the keys

    On the 48K and the Spectrum+ you do not spell out commands: each key types a whole keyword, printed on it. The flashing cursor shows what the next key will give:

    KKeyword: the word on the key. P gives PRINT, R RUN, L LET, G GO TO, F FOR, N NEXT, I INPUT, U IF, K LIST, A NEW, V CLS, B BORDER.
    LLetters, after a keyword. CAPS SHIFT gives capitals; CAPS SHIFT + 2 locks them (cursor C).
    EExtended: press CAPS SHIFT and SYMBOL SHIFT together (Tab on a PC), then a key for the words printed above and below it: E then T gives RND; E then SYMBOL SHIFT + X gives INK, + C gives PAPER, + Z gives BEEP.

    SYMBOL SHIFT (Ctrl or Right Shift) with a key gives the red symbol or word on it: P gives ", L =, K +, J -, B *, V /, F TO, G THEN, Z :, O ;, N , (a comma). On a PC the keys ' , . ; - = / also work. Look at the keyboard on the screen: everything is printed there.

    The words in this chapter

    PRINTPTHENSYMBOL + G
    RUNRTOSYMBOL + F
    LISTKSTOPSYMBOL + A
    NEWA" : ; ,SYMBOL + P Z O N
    LETL= + - * /SYMBOL + L K J B V
    GO TOG( ) $ !SYMBOL + 8 9 4 1
    FOR, NEXTF, NRND, INTEXTEND, then T or R
    INPUT, IFI, UINK, PAPEREXTEND, then SYMBOL + X or C
    CLS, BORDERV, BBEEP, CIRCLEEXTEND, then SYMBOL + Z or H
    PLOT, DRAWQ, WEDITCAPS SHIFT + 1
    SAVE, LOADS, JBREAKCAPS SHIFT + SPACE

    SYMBOL is SYMBOL SHIFT; EXTEND is CAPS SHIFT and SYMBOL SHIFT together (Tab on a PC).

    Your first program

    A line that starts with a number is kept as part of the program. Type each line and press ENTER:

    10 PRINT "HELLO"
    20 GO TO 10

    Then press R (RUN) and ENTER. The screen fills with HELLO; when it asks scroll?, any key carries on and N stops. BREAK (Esc, or CAPS SHIFT + SPACE) stops a program at any time. K (LIST) shows the program, and A (NEW) wipes it.

    Colours

    10 BORDER 1: PAPER 6: INK 2: CLS
    20 PRINT "Red on yellow"

    The colours are numbered 0 to 7 as on the number keys: 0 black, 1 blue, 2 red, 3 magenta, 4 green, 5 cyan, 6 yellow, 7 white. The colon (SYMBOL SHIFT + Z) puts several statements on one line.

    Loops and questions

    10 FOR n=1 TO 10
    20 PRINT n, n*n
    30 NEXT n

    prints the numbers 1 to 10 and their squares. INPUT asks a question; a name ending in $ holds words:

    10 INPUT "Your name? "; a$
    20 PRINT "Hello, "; a$

    IF decides, with THEN (SYMBOL SHIFT + G):

    10 LET x=INT (RND*10)+1
    20 INPUT "Guess 1 to 10: "; g
    30 IF g=x THEN PRINT "Right!": STOP
    40 PRINT "No, try again": GO TO 20

    RND gives a random number between 0 and 1, so x is a whole number from 1 to 10. When you guess it, the program ends with the report 9 STOP statement, 30:3: not an error, just the STOP in line 30.

    Sound and pictures

    BEEP 0.5,0
    BEEP 0.5,7

    plays middle C, then G, for half a second each (the second number is in semitones). Typed without a line number, a command runs at once.

    10 CIRCLE 128,88,60
    20 PLOT 0,0: DRAW 255,175

    draws a circle in the middle of the screen and a line across it; the screen is 256 dots wide and 176 high, counted from the bottom left.

    Changing a program

    To change a line, type it again with the same number; to delete it, type its number alone and press ENTER. Or press EDIT (CAPS SHIFT + 1): the line marked > in the listing comes down to the bottom of the screen, where the arrow keys move along it and DELETE rubs out; ENTER puts it back. The up and down arrows move the > mark between lines.

    When something is wrong, the Spectrum puts a flashing ? where it cannot understand the line, or stops with a report such as 2 Variable not found, 20:1: the error, then the line and the statement where it happened.

    Keeping your program

    Press REC on the recorder (F5), then type

    SAVE "hello"

    (S gives SAVE). Your program goes onto My programs on the rack. To get it back another day: LOAD "" (J, then SYMBOL SHIFT + P twice) and ENTER, then pick the tape when the screen asks.

    On the 128

    Choose 128 BASIC from the menu. There you type every keyword letter by letter (P, R, I, N, T), and the cursor keys move freely around the program. 48 BASIC on the menu works exactly like the 48K.

    7. Settings

    SPEED switchNORMAL: real tape speed, with the border stripes and the loading sound. FAST: the computer runs flat out while the tape plays. INSTANT: programs go straight into memory; games with a loader of their own (turbo, Speedlock and the like) run flat out with the picture still, so they load in seconds too.
    AUTO LOADPutting in a tape resets the computer and starts loading it (LOAD "" on the 48K and Spectrum+, Tape Loader on the 128). On the 128, a game made only for the 48K loads in 48 BASIC, as many of them need. A tape with a 48K and a 128K version starts at the one for your model.
    Games and savingThe catalogue, your libraries, the rack and tapes in parts: chapter 4. Saving and the Multiface: chapter 5.
    CounterShows how far the tape has played; the small button sets it to 000.
    MultifaceThe red button (F4) freezes the game and shows its menu: SAVE, LOAD, TURBO, RESET, RETURN. Choose with the arrows and ENTER, or press S, L, T, X or R; BREAK returns to the game.
    JoystickClick it to plug it in; each click changes the kind, shown on its base: Kempston (most games), Sinclair (Interface 2: keys 6 to 0), Cursor (Protek/AGF: keys 5 to 8, fire 0), then unplugged. Choose the one the game's menu offers. The arrow keys and Left Alt work it. On a phone or tablet a stick appears under your left thumb and a FIRE button under your right. A real joystick or gamepad (USB or Bluetooth) works too: press one of its buttons so the browser sees it; the touch stick then goes away, and its d-pad or stick and main buttons work the kind chosen here.
    TelevisionZOOM (or a double-click on the picture) fills the screen; POWER pauses everything. Volume: drag the VOLUME knob, or roll the mouse wheel anywhere over the TV; click the word VOLUME to turn the sound off and on. Colour: drag the COLOUR knob or hold Shift with the wheel; click the word COLOUR for black and white or a green-screen monitor. The TV remembers its settings, so if you left it silent it stays silent.

    8. About

    An emulator of the Sinclair ZX Spectrum 48K (1982), Spectrum+ (1984) and Spectrum 128 (1986), written in plain JavaScript. It runs in the browser, with nothing to install.

    The Spectrum ROMs are copyright Amstrad, who allow them to be distributed for use with emulators.

    This is a fan project. It is not made, endorsed or supported by Sky, Sinclair Research or Amstrad; ZX Spectrum and Sinclair are trademarks of their owners. Search words typed into the catalogue go to ZXInfo; nothing else leaves your computer.

    The software catalogue searches ZXDB through the ZXInfo service and loads the games from the World of Spectrum archive kept at archive.org. Only titles whose makers allow it can be downloaded.

    A note from the author

    I was a teenager in the 1980s when a ZX Spectrum came into my life. Rubber keys, the family television, long hours typing in program listings from magazines, line by line, hunting for the one mistyped character, and long minutes of screeching tape, waiting to see whether this time the game would load. Those evenings taught me what a computer could be, and a little of who I would become.

    This emulator is my way of switching it on once more. I hope it brings back a few memories for you too.
